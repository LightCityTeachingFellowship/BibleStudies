[4] What Does It Mean To Be Saved?

  1. Salvation Already Received
    1. Scripture speaks of salvation as already received, as presently being worked out, and as still future. These are connected aspects of one salvation [Ephesians 2:5–8; Philippians 2:12–13; 1 Peter 1:5].
    2. We have been saved by grace, according to God’s mercy. This initial salvation is received through faith in Christ, not by completing a prescribed prayer or making a verbal confession [Ephesians 2:8; Titus 3:5].
    3. Repentance and baptism are preached, but a person who believes in Jesus is already saved. While Peter was still speaking to Cornelius and those with him, the Holy Spirit came upon his hearers after the message that everyone who believes in Jesus receives forgiveness of sins [Acts 10:43–48].
    4. The gospel also declares that Jesus is appointed to judge the living and the dead [Acts 10:42].
    5. The forgiveness received in this initial salvation brings reconciliation to God; its key themes include faith, justification, and the death and blood of Jesus [Romans 5:1, 9–11].
  2. Salvation in Progress and Still to Come
    1. Believers are described as those who are being saved and are told to work out their salvation with fear and trembling. Peter describes the salvation of our souls as the goal of faith [1 Corinthians 1:18; Philippians 2:12–13; 1 Peter 1:8–9].
    2. Christ is able to save completely those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to intercede for them. This points to the completion of the salvation received at faith [Hebrews 7:25].
    3. The future and ultimate aspect of salvation is deliverance from God’s wrath and from death. Having been justified and reconciled, we shall be saved through Christ and by His life [Romans 5:9–10; 1 Thessalonians 5:9].
    4. Our salvation is nearer than when we believed. It is ready to be revealed in the last time, and Christ will appear again to bring salvation to those waiting for Him [Romans 13:11; 1 Peter 1:5; Hebrews 9:28].
    5. Jesus speaks of those who endure to the end being saved. The ruler’s question about inheriting eternal life leads to the question of entering God’s kingdom and being saved [Matthew 24:13; Luke 18:18–27].
    6. Not every future form of the word ‘saved’ refers to deliverance from wrath: in the parable of the sower, the devil takes away the word so that people may not believe and be saved. That refers to the beginning of salvation [Luke 8:12].
  3. Faith, Repentance, Works, and Confession
    1. Faith is required throughout salvation, but neither its initial nor its future aspect requires submission to the law of Moses [Ephesians 2:8–9; Galatians 3:23–25].
    2. For initial salvation, repentance is preached but is in practice assumed when a person believes. For the future completion of salvation, repentance must be shown to be genuine; Paul preached that people should repent, turn to God, and prove their repentance by their deeds [Acts 26:20].
    3. Works are not counted as a quantity needed to earn entry. They provide evidence of whether the heart is truly committed to God and to Jesus.
    4. With the heart a person believes and is justified; with the mouth confession is made unto salvation. This confession concerns acknowledging and relating to Jesus as Lord, not necessarily repeating a fixed formula [Romans 10:9–10].
  4. The Death and Life of Jesus
    1. The initial forgiveness and reconciliation depend on the blood and death of Jesus. The future completion of salvation depends on His continuing life and intercession [Romans 5:9–10; Hebrews 7:25].
    2. The death of Jesus cannot be separated in practice from His resurrection. As a priest had to live to bring sacrificial blood into the holy place, Christ must be alive; if He has not been raised, faith is futile and we remain in our sins [Leviticus 16:15; 1 Corinthians 15:16–17].
    3. The repentant man crucified beside Jesus was saved, although he died without water baptism or a continuing earthly life requiring Christ’s intercession [Luke 23:39–43].
  5. God’s Commands and the Law of Moses
    1. Keeping God’s commands matters for Christians. Yet Paul’s statement that circumcision is nothing and keeping God’s commands matters is not a command to submit to the law of Moses [1 Corinthians 7:19].
    2. Believers are not under law but under grace; those led by the Spirit are not under the law. Once faith has come, we are no longer under the law’s supervision [Romans 6:14–15; Galatians 3:23–25; 5:18].
    3. Paul said that he himself was not under the law, although he was not free from God’s law but was under Christ’s law. The law of Moses and the law of Christ both belong to God, but it matters which one governs us [1 Corinthians 9:20–21].
    4. The law was temporary, and the old covenant has been replaced by the new. Paul warned the Galatians against accepting circumcision as submission to the law: the person who does so is obligated to keep the whole law [Galatians 3:19–25; 5:1–4; Hebrews 8:6–13].
  6. The Law Is Good but Is Not the Origin of Righteousness
    1. The law is holy, and its commandment is holy, righteous, and good. It reveals righteous requirements, but it is not the source or beginning of God’s righteousness [Romans 7:12, 16].
    2. Sin existed before the law of Moses. God required righteousness before that covenant, as seen in Noah’s generation and in the command given after the flood concerning human life [Romans 5:13; Genesis 6:5–9; 9:5–6].
    3. The covenant at Horeb was made with Israel, not with its ancestors. Marriage and the prohibition of murder were already present before the law incorporated them [Deuteronomy 5:1–3; Genesis 2:24; 9:6].
    4. A person can learn and follow God’s righteous will without thereby submitting to the law of Moses as a covenant. Paul speaks of an uncircumcised person keeping the law’s righteous requirements and of those who walk by the Spirit fulfilling its righteous requirement [Romans 2:26; 8:4].
    5. The law is good when used lawfully. Read it to learn God’s will, but apply its commands correctly rather than placing yourself under the Mosaic covenant [1 Timothy 1:8].
  7. Learning God’s Will from the Law
    1. Although we are not under the law, we should not discard what it reveals of God’s will. Paul says that through the law he came to know sin, and the New Testament draws on the law when teaching believers [Romans 7:7, 12].
    2. Love for God and love for one’s neighbour summarize the commandments. Their role as a summary shows the importance of the obedience they encompass [Matthew 22:36–40; Romans 13:8–10].
    3. Paul refers to the law when giving instructions for order in the church, including the submission of women. His instruction that a woman not teach or exercise authority over a man is connected to the order of Adam’s and Eve’s creation; this concerns order in the worshipping community, not a prohibition on a woman employing men in business [1 Corinthians 14:34–37; 1 Timothy 2:11–14].
    4. Jesus appealed to the creation account in teaching about marriage and divorce. Genesis records events before the Mosaic covenant, yet it is part of the books of the law [Matthew 19:3–9; Genesis 2:24].
    5. Paul teaches children to honour and obey their parents, quoting the command and its promise that it may go well with them and that they may live long on the earth. The promise was originally given in the law concerning the land God gave Israel [Ephesians 6:1–3; Exodus 20:12].
