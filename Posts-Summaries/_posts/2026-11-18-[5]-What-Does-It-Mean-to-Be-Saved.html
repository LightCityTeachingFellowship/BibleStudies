[5] What Does It Mean to Be Saved?

  1. Key terms in salvation
    1. Righteousness means being considered qualified rather than condemned. It does not mean that a person has never sinned; justification is the act of establishing a person as righteous [Romans 4:5–8].
    2. Reconciliation brings those who were alienated from God back into relationship with Him. Jesus’ death and life are both significant to our salvation [Romans 5:10].
    3. Faith is belief in God and Christ from the heart. Genuine faith seeks God and perseveres [Hebrews 11:6; 10:35–39].
  2. God’s commands and the law of Moses
    1. Keeping God’s commandments matters, but Christians are not to submit to the law of Moses as a covenant. Its covenant observances include circumcision, Sabbaths and food distinctions [1 Corinthians 7:19; Galatians 5:1–4; Colossians 2:16–17].
    2. The same God made both the old and new covenants. We can learn His will from the law without being under it, just as Jesus and the apostles used it to teach and confirm God’s commands [Jeremiah 31:31–32; Matthew 19:4–6; Ephesians 6:1–3].
    3. Covenants have signs: the rainbow was a sign of God’s covenant with Noah, circumcision was a sign of His covenant with Abraham, and the Sabbaths were a sign between God and Israel [Genesis 9:12–17; 17:10–11; Ezekiel 20:12].
    4. One may choose a day of rest as a pattern of life without treating observance of the Sabbath as a covenant obligation.
  3. The righteous requirements of the law
    1. The law and its commandments are holy, just and good, but God—not the law—is the source of righteousness. God demanded righteousness before the law of Moses was given [Romans 7:12; Genesis 4:6–7; 6:9–13].
    2. Marriage and the prohibition of murder preceded the covenant at Sinai. One can therefore follow God’s will in these matters without being under the law of Moses as a covenant [Genesis 2:24; 9:5–6; Matthew 19:4–6].
    3. Paul speaks of an uncircumcised person keeping the righteous requirements of the law. Those who walk according to the Spirit fulfill its righteous requirement [Romans 2:26; 8:3–4].
    4. We should learn God’s will from the law and put it into practice without submitting to its covenant-specific obligations. We must not claim that the Holy Spirit directs us to contradict God’s revealed will.
    5. The law helps us recognize sin. Paul learned what coveting was because the law said not to covet; knowing right and wrong is good, even though sin can exploit that knowledge [Romans 7:7–13].
    6. Love your neighbor as yourself comes from the law of Moses. Jesus and the apostles taught it as central to fulfilling God’s commands [Leviticus 19:18; Mark 12:28–34; Romans 13:8–10].
  4. Learning from promises in the law
    1. Paul teaches children to honor their parents and draws attention to the promise attached to that command. Promises in the old covenant can also instruct and encourage us, though we must understand how they apply to us [Ephesians 6:1–3].
    2. God’s promises do not make prayer unnecessary. Jabez called on the God of Israel to bless him and enlarge his territory, and God granted his request [1 Chronicles 4:9–10].
    3. Use the Scripture you understand in faith and prayer, whether from the Old or New Testament, while continuing to grow in your understanding of both.
  5. The phases of salvation
    1. The unrighteous are deserving of death. Present salvation is from condemnation—the sentence of death—while future salvation is from death itself, including the second death [Romans 1:32; Ephesians 2:1–5; Revelation 20:14–15].
    2. Paul calls the old covenant ministry both a ministry of death and a ministry of condemnation. Forgiveness makes those who were dead in trespasses alive with Christ [2 Corinthians 3:7–9; Colossians 2:13].
    3. These are not two unrelated salvations: both concern deliverance from death. The first phase begins the journey through forgiveness and freedom from condemnation; the future phase completes it.
    4. Jesus died for us, and His continuing intercession secures the forgiveness needed as we journey toward final salvation. His life and intercession are essential to saving us completely [Romans 5:9–10; Hebrews 7:25; 1 John 2:1–2].
  6. Justification, reconciliation and continuing in faith
    1. Through faith, forgiveness brings freedom from condemnation and the standing of righteousness. Because believers still sin, Christ’s intercession remains necessary [Romans 4:5–8; 1 John 2:1–2].
    2. Through the death of God’s Son, those who were enemies are reconciled to God; having been reconciled, they look to be saved through His life [Romans 5:10].
    3. The first phase brings us into God’s family, and Christ’s intercession keeps us in the forgiveness needed to remain there. The promise of life belongs to His children [Romans 8:15–17; Hebrews 7:25].
    4. Faith in God and Christ is required at the beginning. Continuing faith must be shown in repentance and its fruit; a person’s conduct reveals the heart rather than earning salvation by a tally of works [Luke 3:8; 6:43–45; Ephesians 2:8–10].
    5. Do not treat Jesus’ intercession as permission to live however you please. Salvation can be lost; continue faithfully, hate sin and resist it [John 15:1–6; Hebrews 12:3–4].
    6. Jesus promises not to blot the overcomer’s name out of the book of life. Take that promise as a warning to persevere, while trusting God to keep us faithful to the end [Revelation 3:5].
